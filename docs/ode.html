Understanding ODEs Through Ebola Dynamics

Every mathematical model of an infectious disease rests on a fundamental question: how fast is something changing? In the case of Ebola, that "something" is the number of susceptible, infected, recovered, and deceased individuals. An ordinary differential equation (ODE) is the mathematical tool that describes these rates of change precisely.

1. What is an ODE?

An ODE describes how a quantity changes with respect to a single independent variable. In epidemiology, that variable is almost always time. The general form is:

dy/dt = f(y, t)

This reads: "the rate of change of \(y\) with respect to time \(t\) is some function \(f\) of the current value of \(y\) and the current time." If \(y\) is the number of infected people, then \(dy/dt\) is the number of new infections per day minus the number of recoveries and deaths per day.

2. Why ODEs Are Central to Compartmental Modelling

Compartmental models divide a population into groups (S, I, R, D) and track the flow of people between them. Each flow is a rate — which is exactly what an ODE expresses. For example, the flow from Susceptible to Infectious is \(\beta S I / N\), the number of new infections per unit time. That becomes the derivative of \(S\) (with a negative sign, since S decreases) and part of the derivative of \(I\) (with a positive sign, since I increases).

For Ebola, the SIRD system reads:

dS/dt = −β · S · I / N
dI/dt =  β · S · I / N − (γ + μ) · I
dR/dt =  γ · I
dD/dt =  μ · I

Each line is an ODE. Together, they form a system of ordinary differential equations, which is what the deSolve package in R is designed to solve.

3. The Concept of Initial Values

An ODE tells us how a quantity changes, but not where it starts. To reconstruct the full trajectory of an Ebola outbreak, we need initial conditions:

S(0) = 19,999,995     I(0) = 5     R(0) = 0     D(0) = 0

This is called an initial value problem (IVP). The combination of an ODE system and initial conditions uniquely determines the future path of the outbreak.

4. Why We Must Solve Numerically

Very few ODE systems have closed-form analytical solutions. The SIRD system is not one of them, because the term \(S \cdot I\) makes it nonlinear — the compartments feed back into each other in ways that cannot be simplified algebraically. Instead, we must integrate the system numerically, which means stepping forward in tiny increments of time:

S(t + Δt) ≈ S(t) − Δt · β · S(t) · I(t) / N

This is the Euler method. It is the simplest numerical integrator. Production-grade solvers (like lsoda, used by deSolve) use more sophisticated methods that automatically choose the step size, correct for accumulated error, and handle complex situations.

5. The Challenge of Stiffness

Epidemic models are frequently stiff: the variables change on very different time scales. For example, the susceptible population may be tens of millions while the infected population is only a handful — a ratio of \(10^7\) or more. Stiff systems cause naive solvers to fail or require impossibly tiny time steps.

This is why deSolve's default solver (lsoda) is essential. It automatically detects when a system becomes stiff and switches to an implicit solver (like radau) that can handle the wide range of scales without breaking.

6. Relevance to Ebola

In this portal, every time you move the parameter sliders and watch the predictive chart update, you are watching ODEs being solved. The model:

  • Sets initial conditions from the first observed Ebola case in the data.
  • Integrates the SIRD equations forward in time using deSolve.
  • Compares the model's trajectory to the observed cumulative cases.
  • Adjusts the coefficients to minimize the difference (using optim()).
  • Re-integrates forward 14 days to generate the projection.

Without ODEs and numerical integration, none of the predictions, intervention scenarios, or retrospective projections would be possible.

7. Key Takeaways

  • ODEs describe rates, not states — the solution yields states over time.
  • Epidemic models are systems of coupled nonlinear ODEs.
  • Initial conditions shape the entire outbreak trajectory.
  • Ebola models are stiff, so adaptive solvers are essential.
  • Numerical integration replaces the analytical solution in practice.
