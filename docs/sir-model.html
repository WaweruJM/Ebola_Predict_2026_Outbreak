Comprehensive Guide to Compartmental Modelling

Mathematical modelling of infectious diseases, particularly for Ebola, is essential for understanding transmission dynamics, forecasting outbreak trajectories, and evaluating the effectiveness of public health interventions. The foundational framework for this is the compartmental model, which divides a population into distinct groups (compartments) based on their infection status. Individuals flow between these compartments over time.

1. Definition of Key Biological Coefficients

To build a model, we must define the biological parameters that govern how individuals move between compartments. These coefficients are critical and must be estimated from real-world data.

  • Contact Rate (\(c\)): The average number of contacts an infectious individual makes per unit time (e.g., per day).
  • Probability of Transmission given a Contact (\(\tau\)): The likelihood that a contact between an infectious and susceptible individual results in transmission. This depends on the pathogen and the nature of the contact.
  • Transmission Rate (\(\beta\)): The effective contact rate, defined as \(\beta = c \cdot \tau\). It represents the rate at which susceptible individuals become infectious.
  • Recovery Rate (\(\gamma\)): The rate at which infectious individuals recover and move to the Recovered compartment. It is the inverse of the mean infectious period (\(1/D\)).
  • Mortality Rate (\(\mu\)): The rate at which infectious individuals die from the disease and move to the Deceased compartment.
  • Basic Reproduction Number (\(R_0\)): The expected number of secondary cases caused by one infectious individual in a completely susceptible population. For a SIR model with mortality, \(R_0 = \beta / (\gamma + \mu)\). If \(R_0 > 1\), the outbreak grows; if \(R_0 < 1\), it shrinks.

2. Compartmental Models and their Equations

Depending on the disease and the questions being asked, different compartmental structures are used. Below are the most common, applied to Ebola.

2.1 SIS Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Susceptible)

Used when individuals do not develop immunity and return to the susceptible pool after recovery.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} + \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \gamma I \)

2.2 SIR Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Recovered)

The most classic model. Individuals recover and gain permanent immunity.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)

2.3 SIRD Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Recovered – Deceased)

Used when mortality is a key feature. Ebola has a high case fatality rate, so a SIRD model is required to track deaths separately from recoveries.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - (\gamma + \mu) I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dD}{dt} = \mu I \)

2.4 SEIR Model (Susceptible – Exposed – Infectious – Recovered)

Used for diseases with a significant incubation period (latent phase).

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \sigma E \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \sigma E - \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)

2.5 Computing Biological Coefficients from Time-Series Data

In real-world scenarios, the compartment values \(S(t), I(t), R(t), D(t)\) are measured daily (e.g., from reported case counts). The primary unknown values are the biological coefficients (\(\beta, \gamma, \mu, R_0\)). They are derived by integrating the SIRD equations against observed data. Below are the algebraic and computational formulas for each.

1. Mortality Rate (\(\mu\))
\(\mu\) is the rate at which infectious individuals die. It is calculated as the number of deaths divided by the number of active infectious individuals over a time step.

\( \mu = \frac{\Delta D / \Delta t}{I} \)

Alternatively, if the Case Fatality Rate (CFR) and the mean infectious period (\(1/\gamma\)) are known: \(\mu = \frac{\text{CFR}}{1/\gamma}\).

2. Recovery Rate (\(\gamma\))
\(\gamma\) is the rate at which infectious individuals recover. It is derived from the change in the Recovered compartment.

\( \gamma = \frac{\Delta R / \Delta t}{I} \)

Alternatively, \(\gamma = 1 / \text{Mean Infectious Period}\).

3. Transmission Rate (\(\beta\))
\(\beta\) is derived from the rate of new infections (\(\Delta S\)).

\( \beta = \frac{-(\Delta S / \Delta t)}{S \cdot I / N} \)

Alternatively, \(\beta\) can be estimated by fitting the early exponential growth phase of the outbreak.

4. Basic Reproduction Number (\(R_0\))
Once \(\beta, \gamma\), and \(\mu\) are computed, \(R_0\) is calculated directly:

\( R_0 = \frac{\beta}{\gamma + \mu} \)

In practice, daily data is noisy, so we use computational fitting (e.g., non-linear least squares via the optim() function in R). This method iteratively adjusts \(\beta, \gamma\), and \(\mu\) to find the values that minimize the squared difference between the model's predictions and the observed data. This is exactly how the portal's daily pipeline computes the coefficients for Ebola.

5. Modelling, Future Projections, and Utility

Building the model (fitting \(\beta, \gamma, \mu\) to observed data) allows us to trace the epidemic curve. Once calibrated, we can use the model for future projections.

  • Projections: By running the equations forward in time, we can predict the number of cases, hospitalizations, and deaths under current conditions. This gives healthcare providers time to mobilize resources.
  • Informing Preventive Measures: Interventions aim to reduce \(\beta\) (e.g., isolation, safe burials) or increase \(\gamma\) (e.g., rapid treatment). The model allows us to simulate "what if" scenarios to assess the impact of these measures before they are implemented.
  • Posterior (Retrospective) Projections: Modelling is also used to look backward. By analyzing the early phase of an outbreak, we can infer the time and magnitude of the initial animal-to-human (zoonotic) transmission event that seeded the human epidemic. This is crucial for understanding spillover dynamics for future outbreaks.

6. Summary and Relevance to Ebola

For the Ebola outbreak, the SIRD or SEIR models are the most appropriate. They capture the high mortality rate (D) and the significant incubation period (E). The daily computational pipeline of this portal uses these structures to fit the observed data and generate real-time, 14-day forecasts. This provides health-policy leaders with the evidence base needed to plan and evaluate response strategies.
