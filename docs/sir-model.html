Comprehensive Guide to Compartmental Modelling with Interventions

Mathematical modelling of infectious diseases, particularly for Ebola, is essential for understanding transmission dynamics, forecasting outbreak trajectories, and evaluating the effectiveness of public health interventions. The foundational framework for this is the compartmental model, which divides a population into distinct groups based on infection status. This guide extends the classical framework to include time-varying transmission, which is essential for realistic Ebola projections.

1. Definition of Key Biological Coefficients

To build a model, we must define the biological and policy-related parameters that govern how individuals move between compartments.

  • Contact Rate (\(c\)): The average number of contacts an infectious individual makes per unit time (e.g., per day).
  • Probability of Transmission (\(\tau\)): The likelihood that a contact between an infectious and susceptible individual results in transmission.
  • Transmission Rate (\(\beta\)): The effective contact rate, \(\beta = c \cdot \tau\). In this portal, we use a time-varying form: \(\beta(t) = \beta_0 \cdot e^{-k(t - \tau_{\text{start}})}\).
  • Initial Transmission (\(\beta_0\)): The transmission rate before interventions take effect.
  • Intervention Start (\(\tau_{\text{start}}\)): The day (from the first case) at which interventions begin to reduce transmission.
  • Intervention Decay (\(k\)): The rate at which transmission decreases after \(\tau_{\text{start}}\). A larger \(k\) means interventions take effect faster.
  • Recovery Rate (\(\gamma\)): The rate at which infectious individuals recover. It is the inverse of the mean infectious period.
  • Mortality Rate (\(\mu\)): The rate at which infectious individuals die from the disease.
  • Basic Reproduction Number (\(R_0\)): The expected number of secondary cases caused by one infectious individual before interventions: \(R_0 = \beta_0 / (\gamma + \mu)\).

2. Compartmental Models and their Equations

Depending on the disease and the questions being asked, different compartmental structures are used. Below are the most common, applied to Ebola.

2.1 SIS Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Susceptible)

Used when individuals do not develop immunity. Not typically applicable to Ebola.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} + \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \gamma I \)

2.2 SIR Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Recovered)

The most classic model. Individuals recover and gain permanent immunity. Foundation for understanding Ebola, but misses the mortality aspect.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)

2.3 SIRD Model (Susceptible – Infectious – Recovered – Deceased) — Used in This Portal

Used when mortality is a key feature. Ebola has a high case fatality rate, so a SIRD model is required to track deaths separately. In this portal, the transmission rate is time-varying to reflect interventions:

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta(t) S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta(t) S I}{N} - (\gamma + \mu) I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dD}{dt} = \mu I \)
  • \( \beta(t) = \beta_0 \cdot e^{-k \cdot \max(0, t - \tau_{\text{start}})} \)

2.4 SEIR Model (Susceptible – Exposed – Infectious – Recovered)

Used for diseases with a significant incubation period. Ebola has an incubation period of 2–21 days.

  • \( \frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N} \)
  • \( \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \sigma E \)
  • \( \frac{dI}{dt} = \sigma E - \gamma I \)
  • \( \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I \)

2.5 Computing Biological and Intervention Coefficients from Time-Series Data

In real-world scenarios, the compartment values \(S(t), I(t), R(t), D(t)\) are measured daily. The unknown values are the biological and intervention coefficients. They are derived by integrating the SIRD equations against observed data.

1. Mortality Rate (\(\mu\))

\( \mu = \frac{\Delta D / \Delta t}{I} \)

Alternatively, if the Case Fatality Rate (CFR) is known: \(\mu = \frac{\text{CFR}}{1/\gamma}\).

2. Recovery Rate (\(\gamma\))

\( \gamma = \frac{\Delta R / \Delta t}{I} \)

Alternatively, \(\gamma = 1 / \text{Mean Infectious Period}\).

3. Initial Transmission Rate (\(\beta_0\))

Estimated from the early exponential growth phase, before interventions kick in:

\( \beta_0 = \frac{-(\Delta S / \Delta t)_{t=0}}{S_0 \cdot I_0 / N} \)

4. Intervention Start (\(\tau_{\text{start}}\)) and Decay Rate (\(k\))

These are estimated by fitting the SIRD model to the observed data and minimizing the squared error. The fitted curve determines the day when transmission began to decline and how fast it declined.

5. Basic Reproduction Number (\(R_0\))

\( R_0 = \frac{\beta_0}{\gamma + \mu} \)

In practice, daily data is noisy, so we use computational fitting (non-linear least squares via optim() in R). This method iteratively adjusts all coefficients to minimize the difference between model predictions and observed data.

5. Modelling, Future Projections, and Utility

Building the model (fitting \(\beta_0, \gamma, \mu, k, \tau_{\text{start}}\)) allows us to trace the epidemic curve. Once calibrated, we use the model for future projections.

  • Projections: We predict cases, hospitalizations, and deaths under current conditions, giving healthcare providers time to mobilize.
  • Informing Preventive Measures: Interventions reduce \(\beta(t)\). We can simulate the impact of different \(k\) (speed of intervention) and \(\tau_{\text{start}}\) (timing) to evaluate response strategies.
  • Posterior (Retrospective) Projections: By analyzing the early phase, we can infer the time and magnitude of the initial animal-to-human (zoonotic) transmission event that seeded the human epidemic. This is crucial for understanding spillover dynamics for future outbreaks.

6. Summary and Relevance to Ebola

For the Ebola outbreak, the SIRD model with time-varying transmission is the most appropriate. It captures the high mortality rate (D), the intervention effect (\(k, \tau_{\text{start}}\)), and the animal-to-human transmission phase. The daily computational pipeline of this portal uses this structure to fit the observed data and generate real-time, 14-day forecasts. This provides health-policy leaders with the evidence base needed to plan and evaluate response strategies.
